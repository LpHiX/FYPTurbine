The working principles for pump and turbine design can be derived from the conservation of angular momentum by Newton's second law of motion. In turbomachinery, this is usually expressed in terms of the Euler turbine equation shown in \cref{eq:euler_turbine_equation}, where $c$ is velocity in the inertial frame, $w$ is in the rotating frame and $u$ is the local circumferential velocity as shown in \cref{fig:conv-velocity-triangle}.

\begin{equation}
    P=\tau\omega=\rho Q \left(r_2 c_{2u} - r_1 c_{1u}\right)\omega=\rho Q\left(u_2c_{2u}-u_1c_{1u}\right)
    \label{eq:euler_turbine_equation}
\end{equation}




Rewriting this equation in terms of specific work $Y=P/\rho Q$ per unit mass:

\begin{equation}
    Y = \left(r_2 c_{2u} - r_1 c_{1u}\right)\omega = u_2c_{2u}-u_1c_{1u}
     \label{eq:euler_specific_work}
\end{equation}

derive from Bernoulli, with rotating frame substitution $c^2=w^2-u^2+2\times u \times c_u$ and neglecting compressibility losses:

$$p_{1}+\frac{\rho}{2}w_{1}^2-\frac{\rho}{2}u_{1}^2=p_{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}w_{2}^2-\frac{\rho}{2}u_{2}^2$$

and as such the theoretical rise in static pressure:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_{st} = p_2-p_1 = \frac{\rho}{2}\left(w_1^2-w_2^2\right)+\frac{\rho}{2}\left(u_2^2-u_1^2\right)
    \label{eq:pressure_rise}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Non-dimensional Performance and Scaling}
\label{sec:theory_nondim}

Understanding why low specific speed pumps are less efficient, and the design choices that follow from it, comes down to a few non-dimensional parameters so they are defined first. The following derivation uses velocity terms shown visually in \cref{fig:conv-velocity-triangle}. A pump adds energy to the fluid by spinning it up towards the speed of the impeller rim, $u_2$. The work it does per unit mass is the rim speed times the swirl velocity it adds to the fluid, $u_2 c_{2u}$, and that swirl is proportional to the rim speed, so the head a pump can generate scales with $u_2^2$. The flow rate meanwhile leaves the impeller radially through the exit area $\pi d_2 b_2$ at the meridional velocity $c_{m2}$ where $b_2$ is the blade width at the exit, so $Q = \pi d_2 b_2\, c_{m2}$. Measuring the head against the rim-speed head $u_2^2$, and the radial velocity against the rim speed $u_2$, gives the head and flow coefficients, following Gülich \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}:

\begin{equation}
    \psi = \frac{2gH}{u_2^2}, \qquad
    \phi = \frac{c_{m2}}{u_2} = \frac{Q}{\pi d_2 b_2 u_2}
    \label{eq:psi_phi}
\end{equation}

These two coefficients form the non-dimensional performance map of a pump, where the original pump curve $H=f(Q)$ is transformed into $\psi = f(\phi)$. For any geometrically similar pump with the same velocity triangles, the same $\psi(\phi)$ curve applies, so the performance of a small model can be scaled to a larger machine \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}.

\subsubsection{Pump Scaling, Affinity Laws}
\label{sec:theory_scaling}

Using the non-dimensional pump characteristic curve $\psi=f(\phi)$, the performance of a pump can be scaled to different speeds and sizes. At a design flow rate, meaning a fixed $\phi$, the head coefficient $\psi$ is known. This is what makes them useful for scaling. At a fixed point on the $\psi(\phi)$ curve, \cref{eq:psi_phi} can be rearranged:

\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{\psi u_2^2}{2g}, \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad H \propto u_2^2 \propto n^2 d_2^2
    \label{eq:head_scaling}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Q = \phi \pi d_2 b_2 u_2, \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad Q \propto u_2 d_2^2 \propto n d_2^3
    \label{eq:flow_scaling}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Cavitation and NPSH}
Cavitation occurs when the local static pressure of the fluid drops below its vapour pressure, causing the liquid to boil into vapour bubbles. In a pump, this is highly undesirable as the bubbles form and collapse violently repeatedly, eroding the impeller and diffuser surfaces. Additionally, the vapour bubbles are compressible, so they absorb energy that would otherwise be delivered to the fluid, reducing the head and efficiency of the pump. The cavitation behaviour of a pump is characterised by the net positive suction head, NPSH. There are different types of NPSH. At the supply side, $\textrm{NPSH}_a$ stands for net positive suction head available, which the head margin above vapour pressure at the pump inlet. On the pump side, a  $\textrm{NPSH}_r$ is usually specified. Standing for net positive suction head required, it is the minimum NPSH$_a$ at which the pump can operate without cavitation. The standard performance criterion is NPSH$_3$, the available NPSH at which the developed head has dropped 3\% below its fully wetted value at constant speed and flow \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. The formula for the general NPSH is given in \cref{eq:npsh}, where $p_{in}$ is the inlet static pressure, $p_{vap}$ is the vapour pressure of the fluid, $\rho$ is the fluid density, $g$ is gravitational acceleration and $v_{in}$ is the inlet velocity.

\begin{equation}
    \textrm{NPSH} = \frac{p_{in} - p_{vap}}{\rho g} + \frac{v_{in}^2}{2g}
    \label{eq:npsh}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Main Loss Mechanisms at Low Specific Speed}

% The introduction established that this duty sits at low specific speed and that the partial emission pump is the efficiency optimum there. This section explains where that efficiency ceiling comes from, since identifying and measuring the dominant loss is one of the aims of this work.

Losses in pumps can be separated into power losses and head losses. Power losses increase the shaft power required to drive the pump but does not directly affect the head delivered, while head losses directly reduce the head from the ideal value.

\subsubsection{Disc Friction Loss}

When a rotor spins in a fluid, the shear stress in the fluid acts on the rotor faces as a drag torque. In most centrifugal pumps the impeller is broadly disc shaped, so this is called disc friction loss. Taking the main parameters of the equation from Gulich \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}, the power lost to disc friction scales as

\begin{equation}
    P_{RR} \approx k_{RR}\, \rho\, \omega^3 r_2^5
    \label{eq:disc_friction_scaling} % eq 3.6.2 and 3.6.3, simplified
\end{equation}

where $k_{RR}$ is a friction coefficient that falls slowly with Reynolds number. By dividing this by the useful power $P_{hyd}=\rho g Q H$ and substituting the head coefficient $H=\psi u_2^2/2g$ with $u_2=\omega r_2$ gives:

%The important property of \cref{eq:disc_friction_scaling} is that it has no flow rate in it. The disc burns this power whether the pump is delivering flow or is deadheaded against a closed valve. The useful hydraulic power on the other hand is $P_{hyd}=\rho g Q H$, which goes to zero as the flow goes to zero. So the relative size of the disc friction loss is set by how these two compare, and that ratio is where the specific speed enters.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{P_{RR}}{P_{hyd}} \approx \frac{k_{RR}\,\rho\,\omega^3 r_2^5}{\rho g Q H} \approx \frac{k_{RR}\,\omega^3 r_2^5}{g Q}\cdot\frac{2g}{\psi\,\omega^2 r_2^2} = \frac{2 k_{RR}\,\omega r_2^3}{\psi\, Q}
    \label{eq:disc_loss_fraction}
\end{equation}

Then, by substituting the head coefficient into specific speed and rearranging for Q:

\begin{equation}
    n_q = \frac{n\sqrt{Q}}{H^{3/4}} 
\;\propto\; \frac{\omega\sqrt{Q}}{\left(\psi\,\omega^2 r_2^2\right)^{3/4}}
= \frac{\sqrt{Q}}{\psi^{3/4}\,\omega^{1/2} r_2^{3/2}}
\Rightarrow\quad n_q^2 \propto \frac{Q}{\psi^{3/2}\,\omega\, r_2^3}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 
Q \propto n_q^2\,\psi^{3/2}\,\omega\, r_2^3 
\end{equation}

Back into the ratio:

\begin{equation}
\frac{P_{RR}}{P_{hyd}}
\approx \frac{2 k_{RR}\,\omega r_2^3}{\psi\, Q}
\;\propto\; \frac{k_{RR}\,\omega r_2^3}{\psi \cdot n_q^2\,\psi^{3/2}\,\omega\, r_2^3}
= \boxed{\;\frac{k_{RR}}{n_q^{2}\,\psi^{5/2}}\;}
\label{eq:disc_loss_specific_speed}
\end{equation}

\begin{wrapfigure}{R}{0.55\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.54\textwidth]{disc_loss.png}
    \caption{Disc friction loss vs specific speed $n_q$ after Gülich. At low specific speed the disc friction dominates.} % gulich fig 3.21
    \label{fig:disc_loss}
\end{wrapfigure}

This is the reason low specific speed pumps cannot reach high efficiency. The relative disc friction scales as $1/n_q^2$, so halving the specific speed quadruples the share of the input power lost to the disc. As an example point on the size of this, Gülich gives the disc friction as roughly $30\%$ of the useful power of the impeller at $n_q=10$, $\psi\approx1$ and $\mathrm{Re}=10^7$ \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. Gulich provides \cref{fig:disc_loss} to show this relationship visually.


The head coefficient in the denominator of \cref{eq:disc_loss_specific_speed} also points the pump designer to design a pump with a higher head coefficient as $P_{RR}\propto\psi^{-5/2}$. For a fixed desired head $H$ and rotational speed $n$, a high head coefficient means a smaller tip speed and thus a smaller diameter can be derived from \cref{eq:head_coefficient}:

\begin{equation}
    u_2 = \sqrt{\frac{2gH}{\psi}} = \frac{\pi d_2 n}{60}
\end{equation}

Since $P_{RR}\sim r_2^5$ shrinking the diameter collapses the disc friction. This is why Gülich recommends maximising the head coefficient at low specific speed.% and it is exactly what the forced vortex Barske impeller does, reaching $\psi\approx1$ and being pushed a little above it by diffuser recovery.

\subsection{The Barske Partial-Emission Pump}

%barbernichols https://barber-nichols.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/partial_emission_pumps_bridging_efficiency_gap_between_positive_displacement_and_full_emission_pumps.pdf

The Barske pump, also known as the partial-emission pump \cite{barbernichols}, forced vortex pump \cite{lockForcedVortexPump1966} is a type of centrifugal pump found to be effective in these conditions. The Barske Pump is very simple in geometry as shown in \cref{fig:impeller-barske} as compared to a conventional pump such as \cref{fig:impeller-conventional}. The impeller consists of a flat disc with straight radial blades. The volute is swapped for cylindrical (annular) casing, with a divergent diffuser placed tangent to the casing. Because the fluid leaves the impeller at high velocity relative to both the casing and the diffusor, the surface finish becomes a primary concern for efficiency at this scale \cite{lockdesignfeatures}. Gülich also notes that for $n_q<12$ an annular casing can reach higher efficiencies than a volute, the spiral collector of a conventional pump \cite{gulich7.12}, which has the side benefit of being far simpler to manufacture, a real advantage at small scale where tight tolerances are hard to hold. Conversely, as the losses in the impeller are small, the Barske impeller surface finish is not critical and can be left rough.

The Barske impeller pump design which was developed for the need for manufacturing simplicity and low specific speed applications \cite{barskeDevelopmentUnconventionalCentrifugal1960} became one of the main centrifugal pump designs at this specific speed. The Barske impeller operates differently to a normal full emission pump. In a conventional centrifugal pump, impeller blade geometry aims to perfectly guide fluid flow along the blade surfaces in a smooth spiral streamline out of the impeller into the volute \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. The Barske impeller is instead designed to induce almost a pure forced vortex flow where $u=\omega r$ and the fluid is essentially rotating as a solid body with the impeller \cite{barskeDevelopmentUnconventionalCentrifugal1960} where meridional or radially outward flow is negligible \cite{barskeDesignOpenImpeller}. This also explains the unimportance of surface finish on the blades, as low relative velocity between the fluid and the impeller means lower friction losses.
% shrouds stuff page 102 gulich
Another difference between conventional impellers and the barske impeller is the absence of shrouds. The open impeller choice is to further reduce disc friction losses as in the case of radial impellers, shroud friction far exceeds contributions the contribution of all other components \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. Conventional impellers uses shrouds to guide the flow and reduce secondary flows, but as the Barske impeller operates on a wholely different principle, this shroud is not needed. Removing the shrouds also reduces axial thrust loads on the impeller as there is less surface area for the fluid to push against, which assists with sealing and mechanical design of the pump shaft.

\subsubsection{Barske Pump Performance}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.52\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{forced_vortex_triangles}
\caption{Velocity triangles in a forced vortex pump}

To predict the performance of a Barske pump, Barske starts by assuming that the flow is nearly a pure forced vortex as shown in \cref{fig:pump-velocity-triangle}. He also assumes the relative velocity $w$ is assumed to be negligible. This turns \cref{eq:pressure_rise} into \cref{eq:pressure_rise_negligible_w}, which is the theoretical static pressure rise of a Barske impeller.

\begin{equation}
    \Delta p_{st} = \frac{\rho}{2}\left(u_2^2-u_1^2\right)
    \label{eq:pressure_rise_negligible_w}
\end{equation}

The kinetic energy increase assuming negligible inlet prerotation is:

\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{\rho}{2}u_2^2
    \label{eq:pressure_rise_negligible_w_prerotation}
\end{equation}

\cref{eq:pressure_rise_negligible_w_prerotation} is the theoretical dynamic pressure a barske impeller generates at the blade tips. In a barske pump, the diffuser is responsible for converting this kinetic energy into static pressure by slowing down the fluid in an expanding diffuser. Barske introduces a loss coefficient for the kinetic energy which can be recovered through the diffuser to be between 0.2 and 0.5. Barske uses the symbol $\psi$ for this coefficient, but as it conflicts with the commonly used symbol for head coefficient, this paper will instead use $k_d$. Combining the static and kinetic pressure rise, the total increase in pressure is:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta p = \Delta p_{st} + k_d\, q=\frac{1}{2}\rho\left((1+k_d) u_2^2 - u_1^2\right)
    \label{eq:barske_pressure_rise}
\end{equation}

This simple model is used to generate the dimensions in \cref{sec:theory_barske_sizing}, but a more refined model from Lock \cite{lockForcedVortexPump1966} is described in \cref{sec:theory_lock} to more accurately predict the full characteristics of the pump at any operating point.

\subsubsection{Barske Sizing Method}
\label{sec:theory_barske_sizing}

The barske pump is sized by the methods provided by barske \cite{barskeDesignOpenImpeller} with the assistance of Gulich. First the inlet is sized by choosing a reasonable inlet velocity and calculating the inlet area:

\begin{equation}
    d_0 = \sqrt{\frac{4\dot{m}}{\pi \rho c_0}}
    \label{eq:barske_inlet_diameter}
\end{equation}

The impeller blade inlet diameter is enlarged by a small amount as some pre-rotation is desirable for increased head. For example, $d_1\sim1.1d_0$.

By substituting $u_2=\pi n d_2 / 60$ into \cref{eq:barske_pressure_rise} and solving for $d_2$, the required impeller tip diameter can be calculated from mass continuity:

\begin{equation}
    d_2 = \sqrt{\frac{1}{1+k_d}\left(\frac{7200\Delta p}{\rho n^2\pi^2} + d_1^2\right)}
    \label{eq:barske_tip_diameter}
\end{equation}

Barske then gives these guidelines for other dimensions of the pump:

\begin{equation}
    s_{ax} \leq \min{\left(0.75, \frac{d_2}{100}\right)} \qquad
    b_1 \geq \frac{1}{4} d_1 \qquad
    b_2 \geq \max{\left(b_1 \frac{d_1}{d_2}, 3s_{ax}\right)}
    \label{eq:blade_height}
\end{equation}

For the annular casing sizing, Gulich's guideline where B/H = 1.5 to 2.5 can be followed:

\begin{equation}
    B = 2 s_{ax} + b_2 \qquad
    H = B / 2
\end{equation}

To size the diffuser, Barske suggests sizing the diffuser throat to be around 0.8 to 0.9 of $u_2$, with the area of the diffuser outlet to be 3-4 times the area of the diffuser throat.

\subsubsection{Efficiency and Disc Friction}

Barske assumes that the disc friction losses can be approximated as a circular disc in a rotating fluid. Converting the equation into metric units gives:

\begin{equation}
    P_{RR} [\mathrm{W}] = 1956\times\rho\nu^{0.2}\left(\frac{n}{1000}\right)^{2.8}\left(d_2^{4.6}+4.6d_1^{3.6}b_1\right)
\end{equation}

As the hydraulic efficiency is defined as the useful power out of the pump over the total power into the pump, efficiency can be defined as:

\begin{equation}
\eta_h  = \frac{\text{Useful power}}{\text{Input power}} = \frac{P}{P_{th}+P_{RR}}
  = \frac{Q\Delta p}{Q\Delta p_{th}+P_{RR}}
  = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta p_{th}+P_{RR}/Q}
\end{equation}

If total shaft efficiency is desired, the mechanical losses $P_m$ can be included:

\begin{equation}
    \eta=\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta p_{th}+(P_{RR}+P_m)/Q}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Lock's Refined Model}
\label{sec:theory_lock}

Lock provides a refined model which takes into account prerotation, nozzle losses and correction coefficients taking into account theoretical two dimensional flow solutions by A. Busemann and Wislicenus \cite{lockForcedVortexPump1966,wislicenusFluidMechanicsTurbomachinery1947}. Lock uses busemann's corrections and derives a refined total head generated by the impeller $H_{T2}$ as \cref{eq:lock_total_head}, where $C_H$ and $h_0$ are the Buseman correction factors taken from his figure 10A and 10B respectively. $K_1$ is the prerotation factor which he says himself is arbitrary, and $Q_{opt}$ is the optimum flow rate at which the pump operates at maximum efficiency.

\begin{equation}
    H_{T2} = h_0\frac{u_2^2}{g} - C_H\,K_1\!\left(1-\frac{Q}{Q_{opt}}\right)\!\frac{d_1}{d_2}\,\frac{u_2^2}{g}
    \label{eq:lock_total_head}
\end{equation}

He then uses a diffuser loss coefficient $\zeta_D=0.194$ to account for diffuser lossess $H_{NL}$ as shown in \cref{eq:lock_diffuser_loss}. This coefficient was taken from the Hydraulic Institute's Pipe Friction Manual \cite{hydraulicinstitutePipeFrictionManual1954}.

\begin{equation}
H_{NL} = \zeta_{D}\left[1-\left(\tfrac{d_3}{d_4}\right)^2\right]^2\frac{c_3^2}{2g}
\label{eq:lock_diffuser_loss}
\end{equation}

Finally, to find the static head developed across the pump, the total head generated by the diffuser $H_{T2}$ is reduced by the diffuser losses $H_{NL}$, dynamic head at the diffuser exit $c_4^2/2g$ and the dynamic head at the impeller inlet $c_0^2/2g$ as shown in \cref{eq:lock_static_head}.

\begin{equation}
H = H_{T2} - H_{NL} - \frac{c_4^2}{2g} + \frac{c_0^2}{2g}
\label{eq:lock_static_head}
\end{equation}

Equation \cref{eq:lock_total_head} requires the parameter $Q_{opt}$, which is solved by expressing \cref{eq:lock_static_head} as a function of flow rate $Q$ and taking the derivative with respect to $Q$ and setting it to zero. By expressing $c^2/2g$ by $(8/g\pi^2)Q^2/d^4$ with $c=4Q/\pi d^2$, the head can be expressed as a function of flow rate $Q$ as shown in \cref{eq:lock_static_head_flow}.

\begin{equation}
    H = h_0\frac{u_2^2}{g} - C_H\,K_1\!\left(1-\frac{Q}{Q_{opt}}\right)\!\frac{d_1}{d_2}\,\frac{u_2^2}{g} - \frac{8Q^2}{g\pi^2}\left(\frac{\zeta_{D}}{d_3^4} + \frac{1}{d_4^4} - \frac{1}{d_0^4}\right)
    \label{eq:lock_static_head_flow}
\end{equation}

Solving the derivative for $Q_{opt}$ gives the optimum flow rate as shown in \cref{eq:lock_optimum_flow}.

\begin{equation}
    Q_{opt} = \sqrt{\frac{h_0\ u_2^2}{\frac{24}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{\zeta_{D}}{d_3^4}\left(1-\left(\frac{d_3}{d_4}\right)^2\right) + \frac{1}{d_4^4} - \frac{1}{d_0^4}\right)}}
    \label{eq:lock_optimum_flow}
\end{equation}

Lock and Barske suggest that the flow cutoff point is limited by cavitation at the diffuser throat. To find the static pressure at the diffuser throat, reversing the transfer between dynamic head into static head provided by the previous equations:

\begin{equation}
    H_3 = H + \frac{8Q^2}{g\pi^2}\left(\frac{\zeta_{D}}{d_3^4}\left(1-\left(\frac{d_3}{d_4}\right)^2\right) + \frac{1}{d_4^4} - \frac{1}{d_3^4}\right)
    \label{eq:lock_diffuser_throat}
\end{equation}

Then solving for the $Q$ at which $H_3$ is equal to vapor pressure head $H_{vap}=\frac{1}{\rho g}\left(p_{vap}-p_{inlet}\right)$ gives the flow cutoff point.

An observation by Lock should be kept in mind when his model is compared to data later. He notes that rounding of the peripheral leading edges reduces the effective head \cite{lockForcedVortexPump1966}. This is evidently not captured in the model and could one of the many reasons for discrepancies between theory and experiment.


\subsection{Turbine Theory}

\subsubsection{Impulse Turbines}
\label{subsec:impulse_turbines}

Turbines work with the same Euler turbine equation. In the supersonic impulse turbine design, the blades are only occupy a small outer portion of the blisk ($d_3\approx d_4\approx d_{\textrm{mean}}$, $u_3\approx u_4\approx u$), so the euler turbine equation \cref{eq:euler_turbine_equation} can be simplified:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta h_{\textrm{use}}=c_{3u}u_3-c_{4u}u_4=u(c_{3u}-c_{4u})
    \label{eq:turbine_specific_work}
\end{equation}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.6\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.58\textwidth]{turbine_cantilever.pdf}
    \caption{Impulse turbine velocity triangle nomenclature at the rotor inlet (station 3) and exit (station 4).} % PLACEHOLDER IMAGE: generic labelled triangles, or merge with fig:turbine-triangles-design
    \label{fig:turbine-velocity-triangles}
\end{wrapfigure}

This following derivation follows from Sudhof's \cite{sudhofDevelopmentTechniquesSupersonic2020}. From \cref{eq:turbine_specific_work}, it is understood that a higher flow velocity difference $c_{3u}-c_{4u}$ per unit mass will lead to a higher specific work $\Delta h_{use}$ according to equation \cref{eq:euler_specific_work}. Maximizing $c_{3u}-c_{4u}$ as visualized by \cref{fig:turbine-velocity-triangles} is done by maximizing the absolute velocity and as close to $180^\circ$ of turning as possible. For this reason, gas generator cycle turbines tend to take advantage of the high pressure ratios and thus supersonic velocities. The amount of flow turning is usually limited by geometrical and mechanical design reasons.

\subsubsection{Impulse Turbine Efficiency}

The ideal efficiency of the impulse stage follows from \cref{eq:turbine_specific_work} and the velocity triangles in \cref{fig:turbine-velocity-triangles}. At the rotor inlet and exit the circumferential components of the absolute and relative velocities are related by

\begin{equation}
    c_{3u} = w_{3u} + u, \qquad -w_{4u} = -c_{4u} + u \Rightarrow c_{4u} = u + w_{4u}
    \label{eq:triangle_components}
\end{equation}

The ideal impulse blade turns the relative flow without slowing it down, so the exit relative velocity mirrors the inlet one, $w_{4u} = -w_{3u}$. Substituting into \cref{eq:turbine_specific_work},

\begin{equation}
    \Delta h_{\textrm{use}} = u\left(c_{3u} - c_{4u}\right)
    = u\left[(w_{3u}+u) - (u+w_{4u})\right]
    = 2u\, w_{3u}
    = 2u\left(c_{3u} - u\right)
    \label{eq:impulse_work}
\end{equation}

As ideally, nozzle exit circumferential velocity equals the circumferential velocity at the blade inlet, $c_{2u}=c_{3u}$, \cref{eq:impulse_work} gives the required circumferential velocity the nozzle must deliver for a given specific work $\Delta h_{\textrm{use}}$ and blade speed $u$. The nozzle exit absolute velocity $c_3$ is related to the circumferential component by the nozzle angle $\beta_3$ measured from the circumferential direction as shown in \cref{eq:triangle_inversion}.

\begin{equation}
    c_{3u} = \frac{\Delta h_{\textrm{use}}}{2u} + u, \qquad
    c_3 = \frac{c_{3u}}{\cos\beta_3}, \qquad
    c_{3m} = c_3 \sin\beta_3
    \label{eq:triangle_inversion}
\end{equation}

Dividing the work \cref{eq:impulse_work} by the kinetic energy $c_3^2/2$ the nozzle supplies gives the stage efficiency. In a pure impulse stage the entire expansion happens in the nozzle, so the available energy per unit mass is the jet kinetic energy $c_3^2/2$, and with $c_{3u} = c_3\cos\beta_3$.

\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\textrm{ideal}}
    = \frac{2u\left(c_3\cos\beta_3 - u\right)}{c_3^2/2}
    = 4\,\frac{u}{c_3}\left(\cos\beta_3 - \frac{u}{c_3}\right)
    \label{eq:impulse_ideal_efficiency}
\end{equation}

The efficiency depends only on the blade to jet speed ratio $u/c_3$ and the nozzle angle. By taking the derivative of \cref{eq:impulse_ideal_efficiency} and setting it to zero, the optimum sits at $u/c_3 = \cos\beta_3/2$. For most turbines with a low $\beta_3$ the optimum is around $u/c_3\approx0.5$.


% , where the exit swirl is zero and the efficiency peaks at $\cos^2\beta_3$. The same expression with the Weiss loss coefficients of \cref{subsec:weissloss} attached becomes $\eta_h = 2k_N^2\,(u/c_3)\left(\cos\beta_3 - u/c_3\right)(1+k_R)$, which reduces to \cref{eq:impulse_ideal_efficiency} when the nozzle and rotor are loss free ($k_N = k_R = 1$); this lossy form is the solid curve in \cref{fig:turbine-eta-uc}.

The jet spouting velocity $c_3$ used in this ratio is the spouting velocity, the velocity reached by an ideal isentropic expansion of the ideal gas over the full stage pressure ratio,

\begin{equation}
    c_3 = \sqrt{2\,\Delta h_{1\rightarrow 3}}
    = \sqrt{2\, c_p T_{01} \left[1 - \left(\frac{p_3}{p_{01}}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]}
    \label{eq:spouting_velocity}
\end{equation}

For 300 K air with a pressure ratio of 10, $c_3 = \valTurbexCThree$. A 100 mm diameter turbine on a 20,000 RPM shaft has a blade speed of around \valTurbexU, leading to a $u/c_3$ of \valTurbexUCThree. The spouting velocity of even a moderate pressure ratio gas generator at a higher outlet temperature can quickly reach up to velocities $c_3$ from 800 m/s upwards, so $u/c_3$ is forced far below the optimum of around 0.5. This is a fundamental limitation of small diameter turbines.

\subsubsection{Blade Height and Partial Admission}

Another problem with designing small turbines is with the low blade height required. By calculating the mass flux through the turbine, the blade height can be designed:

\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{\dot{m}}{\rho_3 c_{3m} d_{\textrm{mean}}}
    \label{eq:turbine_blade_height_full_admission}
\end{equation}

Where $\rho_3$ is the density of the turbine gas at the turbine inlet calculated using isentropic gas relations. Using the example turbine above (a 2 kW stage with $\dot{m}=\valTurbexMdot$ at $\rho_3=\valTurbexRhoThree$), the required full admission blade height would be \valTurbexHeightFull, which is far too small to manufacture or to run without the wall boundary layers swallowing the whole passage. Blade height as small as 1.25 mm on a turbine diameter of 50 mm has been reported to work in practice by \cite{weissCustomizedORCMicro2020}. This is in line with their internal design rules, where a blade height to mean diameter of $h/d_{\textrm{mean}}$ from 0.025 to 0.10 is recommended. However, NASA's SP-8107 recommends a minimum height of 3.8 mm \cite{TurbopumpSystemsLiquid1974}.  By introducing partial admission, the mass flow through the turbine can be restricted to a smaller arc around the annulus at a slight cost to efficiency. The partial admission ratio $\zeta$ can be included into the blade height formula as so:

\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{\dot{m}}{\zeta \rho_3 c_{3m} d_{\textrm{mean}}}
    \label{eq:turbine_blade_height}
\end{equation}

By reducing the admission to $\zeta=\valTurbexZeta$, the same mass flow is forced through a smaller arc of the annulus and the blade height is increased to \valTurbexHeightPartial. The blade height matters because it sets the blade aspect ratio $H/c$, the blade height over chord, which fixes how much of the passage the endwall boundary layers block. 

Ohlsson found that blade efficiency and nozzle efficiency both drop with low aspect ratio blades. He tested 4 turbines with aspect ratios ranging from 0.07 and 0.7 on a 4 inch diameter turbine. He found that the efficiency of both the nozzle and the rotor dropped linearly to the inverse of the aspect ratio, as shown in \cref{eq:ohlsson_efficiency} \cite{ohlssonLowAspectRatio1964}.

\begin{equation}
\eta_{\textrm{nozzle}} = 0.92 - 0.016(c_{\textrm{nozzle}}/H_{\textrm{nozzle}}) \qquad \eta_{\textrm{rotor}} = 0.91 - 0.10(c_{\textrm{rotor}}/H_{\textrm{rotor}})
\label{eq:ohlsson_efficiency}
\end{equation}

This loss of efficiency with low aspect ratio has to be balanced with the loss of efficiency with partial admission. Weiss provides \cref{eq:weiss_ventilation} for the power loss having to drag the non-admitted arc through stagnant air as a function of partial admission ratio $\zeta$ and blade height $H$. The other loss present only in partial admission turbines is the loss associated with the filling and emptying the blade channels. Ohlsson found that having a single continuous arc of admission was more efficient than distributing arcs of admission around the annulus as the entering loss is repeated \cite{ohlssonPartialAdmissionTurbines1962}. NASA SP-8110 agrees with this, but also notes that sometimes this continuous nozzle arc has to be violated if designs cannot sustain the uneven loads \cite{LiquidRocketEngine1974}.

\subsubsection{Loss Model}
\label{subsec:weissloss}

The main loss model used in the design of the turbine is the one provided by Weiß \cite{weiss}. There are more general loss models provided by texts such as NASA SP-290 \cite{glassmanTurbineDesignApplication1994}, giving loss models for tip-clearance, disk friction, partial admission and incidence losses. However, those models were built on large full-admission, subsonic to transonic turbines. Weiss specifically derived his loss model for the specialized application of a supersonic impulse turbines at small scales (1-200kW) \cite{weissCustomizedORCMicro2020}. As the regime of the turbine considered in this thesis matches closer to Weiss's model compared to SP-290's model, Weiss's model is used. Weiss describes different models lead to stage efficiency differences of about 10\% points. Derived from rocket turbopumps literature and adjusted by in-house measurements, Weiss provides three empirical relations to form his loss model. Weiss references Traupel \cite{traupelThermischeTurbomaschinenZweiter2013}, Craig \cite{craigPerformanceEstimationAxial1970a} and Kacker's \cite{kackerMeanLinePrediction1982} models for these larger subsonic full admission turbines.

\begin{equation}
    k_N = \sqrt{1 - \left( 0.0029\,M_3^3 - 0.0502\,M_3^2 + 0.2241\,M_3 - 0.0877 \right)}
    \label{eq:weiss_nozzle_coeff}
\end{equation}

\cref{eq:weiss_nozzle_coeff} is the nozzle loss coefficient, relating the actual velocity to the theoretical isentropic velocity by $k_N=c_{No}/c_{No,is}$. 

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        k_R ={}& 0.957
            - 3.62\times10^{-4}\,\Delta\beta
            - 2.58\times10^{-2}\,M_{w3} \\
            &+ 6.39\times10^{-6}\,\Delta\beta^2
            + 6.74\times10^{-2}\,M_{w3}^2 \\
            &- 7.53\times10^{-8}\,\Delta\beta^3
            - 4.30\times10^{-2}\,M_{w3}^3 \\
            &- 2.38\times10^{-4}\,\Delta\beta\, M_{w3}
            + 1.45\times10^{-6}\,\Delta\beta^2 M_{w3}
            + 4.25\times10^{-5}\,\Delta\beta\, M_{w3}^2
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:weiss_rotor_coeff}
\end{equation}

\cref{eq:weiss_rotor_coeff} is the loss of velocity along the blade passage through a theoretically impulse blade, relating the actual velocity to the theoretical velocity by $k_R=w_{exit}/w_{inlet}$.

\begin{equation}
    P_v = \frac{1.85}{2}\,(1 - \zeta)\,\rho_3 \left( \frac{n}{60} \right)^{3} d_m^4 \,(4.5\,H)
    \label{eq:weiss_ventilation}
\end{equation}

Finally, \cref{eq:weiss_ventilation} captures the losses due to partial admission. Weiss calls this loss ventilation losses, but it can also be called windage losses. Ventilation losses refers to the non-admitted arc being dragged through stagnant air.

% The choice of Weiss's model over the classical decompositions is a regime match rather than a claim that one is better. The losses that dominate a small supersonic partial admission impulse stage are supersonic friction in the nozzle and blade passage plus ventilation, which is exactly what Weiss's coefficients parameterise. The classical loss breakdowns such as Glassman's are anchored in large full admission machines where tip clearance and incidence dominate \cite{glassmanTurbineDesignApplication1994}, and those losses are largely absent here by architecture, since the blades are shrouded and the velocity triangles are matched at the design point. Whether the Weiss prediction is actually trustworthy is checked against measured data from a machine of the same architecture in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Turbine Blade Design}

The design so far is based on 1D mean line theory. The design of the actual blade profile is what will guide the supersonic flow from the inlet direction to the outlet direction to maximize $c_{3u}-c_{4u}$. The basis of supersonic impulse blade profiling is often accomplished using an application of the Method of Characteristics (MOC) to transition a uniform flow field into vortex flow.

\subsubsection{Method of Characteristics for Supersonic Impulse Blade Design}
\label{subsec:method_of_characteristics}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.55\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{moc_goldman.png}
    \caption{Typical MOC blade terminology.}
    \label{fig:turbine-blade-terminology}
\end{wrapfigure}

 Vortex flow is a special type of supersonic flow in which every streamline follows a concentric path around a central point. This flow field was first discovered by A. Busemann \cite{colcloughDesignTurbineBlades1966}, achieved by conditions where expansion and compression waves cancel each other to allow shockless rotation of the flow field. The vortex flow is bounded by the two circular arcs of the surfaces of which a desired surface Mach number is achieved. Boxer then first developed the method to transition uniform flow into vortex flow with MOC \cite{boxerApplicationSupersonicVortexflow1952}. This method generates the profile for an inlet Mach number, turning angle and the mach numbers on the blade surfaces. Later analysis will need to be required to choose these desired Mach numbers.



Turbine blade terminology is based on the blade's upper and lower surfaces as shown in Figure \ref{fig:turbine-blade-terminology}. The top surface is the convex one, and the bottom surface as the concave one. This is in relation to the solid blades themselves as opposed to an alternate designation by the top and bottom of the blade passage. Supersonic vortex flow satisfies the equation:

\begin{equation}
    cr = \textrm{constant}
    \label{eq:vortex_condition}
\end{equation}

Boxer \cite{boxerApplicationSupersonicVortexflow1952} and Goldman \cite{goldmanAnalyticalInvestigationSupersonic1968} nondimensionalizes \cref{eq:vortex_condition} using the critical velocity $M^{*}=c/a^*$ where $a^*=\sqrt{\gamma R T^*}$ is the speed of sound at the sonic point. As $a^*$ only depends on stagnation temperature, it is constant along any streamline of fixed stagnation enthalpy. Then, a nondimensional radius $r^*$ is introduced as the sonic velocity streamline in the vortex field, giving the following condition:

\begin{equation}
    M^* r^* = 1
    \label{eq:vortex_condition_nondim}
\end{equation}

Goldman gives an equation \cite{goldmanAnalyticalInvestigationSupersonic1968} to find the Prandtl-Meyer angle from the critical mach number $M^*$ as:

\begin{equation}
\nu(M) = \frac{\pi}{4}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}} - 1\right) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}} \arcsin\left[(\gamma-1)M^{*2} - \gamma\right] + \arcsin\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{M^{*2}} - \gamma\right)\right)
\label{eq:prandtl_meyer_angle}
\end{equation}

And the converting between Mach number and Critical Mach number is given by:

\begin{equation}
M^* = \sqrt{\frac{(\gamma+1)M^2/2}{1 + (\gamma-1)M^2/2}}
\label{eq:mach_to_critical_mach}
\end{equation}

The rest of the methodology to design the blade profile can be found in Goldman's paper \cite{goldmanAnalyticalInvestigationSupersonic1968} and will not be repeated here. Equations \cref{eq:vortex_condition_nondim} and \cref{eq:mach_to_critical_mach} are highlighted as they can be used to derive the nondimensional radius of the upper and lower surfaces of the blade, which will be important in the following discussion on surface Mach number selection. It is important to note that the inlet mach number for starting here is computed not with the absolute velocity $c_3$ but with the relative velocity $w_3$, as the flow will be turning inside the rotating frame of the turbine blade. This distinction is made as the relative velocity is dependent on the rotational speed of the turbine, meaning that this blade profiling is optimized for only the design point of the turbine. The surface Mach numbers are constrained by two mechanisms. Flow separation and supersonic starting.

\subsubsection{Flow Separation}
\label{subsec:flow_separation}

Separation occurs in high adverse pressure gradients. In supersonic flow, this happens whenever the flow is decelerating along the surface.  There are two main regions on the blade surfaces which separation can occur. At the upper surface, the inlet transition arc accelerates the flow from the inlet velocity until the vortex flow, then decelerates the flow until the outlet. At the lower surface, the inlet transition arc decelerates the flow from the inlet velocity until the vortex flow, then accelerates the flow until the outlet.  Goldman believes that separation on the lower surface is probably not as important as the flow would tend to reattach shortly as the pressure gradient becomes favourable again \cite{goldmanAnalyticalInvestigationBlade1970}. Goldman uses the value of the incompressible form factor $H_i$ along the blade surface to predict separation, where Schlichting \cite{schlichtingBoundaryLayerTheory2017} found that separation usually occurs in the range of $H_i$ from 1.8 to 2.4. The variation of $H_i$ along the blade surface can be seen in \cref{fig:goldman-hi-paper}.

Sasman and Cresci \cite{sasmanCompressibleTurbulentBoundary1966} provides a method to compute the development of the compressible turbulent boundary layer and thus find the incompressible form factor $H_i$ along both surfaces. \subsubsection{Supersonic Starting}
\label{subsec:supersonic_starting}

On startup before the turbine passage runs supersonically, a detached bow shock stands ahead of the leading edges. The flow behind this normal shock is subsonic and has lost a significant amount of total pressure. For the turbine to operate nominally with supersonic flow, the passage must be able to "swallow" this shock, allowing the shock to move through the whole passage. \todo{make sure this explains at standstill}

To do so, the post shock flow with a reduced total pressure must have enough of a flow area pass the required mass flow similar to the choked flow condition. A similar condition is the unstart of a supersonic wind tunnel or a supersonic internal intake. However, Boxer notes that due to the large velocity gradient between the channel walls due to the turning, the flow cannot be assumed one directional as are the case in the provided examples. Goldman provides the condition \cite{goldmanAnalyticalInvestigationSupersonic1968} accounting for the "reduction of mass flow due to curvilinear flow" to calculate the maximum inlet design mach number to ensure starting as:

\begin{equation}
\frac{p_{02}}{p_{01}}(M_i) \;\geq\; \frac{Q}{1-C}
\label{eq:goldman_starting}
\end{equation}

Where $p_{02}/p_{01}$ is the total pressure loss across the normal shock, which is a function of $M_i$, the inlet design mach number. The right side of equation \eqref{eq:goldman_starting} involves nondimensional parameters $Q$ and $C$ which can be solved for through equation 27, 34a and 34b as a function of the surface mach numbers $M^*_l$ and $M^*_u$. $Q$ is named the vortex parameter \cite{sudhofDevelopmentTechniquesSupersonic2020} and $C$ is the reduction in mass flow due to two-dimensional flow. Solving this equation for $M_i$ gives the maximum inlet design mach number to ensure starting. Once again, this maximum inlet mach number is not based on absolute velocity $c_3$ but on the relative velocity $w_3$. The relative inlet mach number at the design point is lower than at startup, as $w_{3u}=c_{3u}-u$ following the velocity triangle shown in \cref{fig:turbine-velocity-triangles}. The starting condition at \cref{eq:goldman_starting} should be more conservatively evaluated at standstill to ensure that the turbine will start at all rotational speeds.

\subsubsection{Surface Mach Number Selection}

The separation criterion described in \cref{subsec:flow_separation} limits the acceleration of the flow along the upper surface, and the deceleration of the flow along the lower surface. Then, the starting criterion described in \cref{subsec:supersonic_starting} provides an additional limit on the inlet design mach number based on these surface mach numbers. 

However, Goldman notes that for optimum efficiency, the maximum spread of surface number leads to maximum efficiencies \cite{goldmanAnalyticalInvestigationBlade1970}. It comes from the fact that a loss of velocity across the impulse blade leads to a loss of kinetic energy transfer to the blades, so he posits the efficiency of an impulse blade to be:

\begin{equation}
    \eta = \left(\frac{w_{exit}}{w_{exit,ideal}}\right)^2
    \label{eq:goldman_efficiency}
\end{equation}

\cref{eq:goldman_efficiency} is nearly the same as Weiss' \cref{eq:weiss_rotor_coeff} loss. Weiss derived his empirical model only of 1D parameters such as inlet mach number and the angled the flow is turned. What this efficiency tries to capture is the boundary layer friction losses, shocks, and exit wake mixing losses. The effect of spreading the surface mach numbers becomes clear by looking at the vortex equation \cref{eq:vortex_condition_nondim}. The larger the mach number spread, the larger the difference in $R^*$ between the upper and lower surface, the larger physical passage width as shown in \cref{fig:moc-spread}. The wider the channel compared to the blade chord, the lower fraction of the boundary layer thickness to passage width, the lower the wetted surface area per unit flow, and thus the lower the losses. This is the challenge of supersonic turbine blade design, maximum efficiency is achieved by riding along the edge of separation and starting.

\subsubsection{Finite Leading Edge}
\label{subsec:finite_leading_edge}

In \cref{subsec:method_of_characteristics}, the upper and lower surface are mathematically designed to produce no shocks in transitioning the uniform flow from the nozzle exit into the vortex flow. For no shocks to form, both surfaces must be perfectly parallel to the starting flow. This is problematic because this implies the point where the two surfaces intersect at the leading edge, the angle between the upper and lower surfaces is zero. This creates an impossible to manufacture zero thickness leading edge. Boxer suggests a few methods to solve this issue. He separates design into turbines with a subsonic or supersonic axial velocity component. 

Boxer \cite{boxerApplicationSupersonicVortexflow1952} describes a few finite leading edge treatments depending on the axial velocity component. For subsonic axial velocity turbines, either lower or upper surface can be designed with an incidence angle to the inlet flow. Angling the upper convex surface and keeping the lower concave surface parallel to the inlet flow, the compressive oblique shock will be generated along the region between the inlet and the nozzle exit, affecting the flow in the next blade passage. Kantrowitz shows that in steady state a compression shock of equal strength will be created to obtain the steady state condition \cite{kantrowitzSupersonicAxialFlowCompressor1950}. To solve this, the lower concave surface can be angled so that the shock stays within the blade passage and does not affect the upstream flow as shown in \cref{fig:finite-le}. The oblique compression shock can then be designed to intersect the upper surface at an angle to fully cancel out the shock. For supersonic axial velocity turbines, a wedge can be designed on the convex surface with a low enough angle from the design inlet flow so that the shock stays within the blade passage and can be cancelled out by a kink on the concave surface.

\subsubsection{Nozzle Design}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.5\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{nozzle_eff.png}
    \caption{Calculated efficiency of a conical nozzle as a function of area ratio from NASA SP-8120 \cite{LiquidRocketEngine1976}}
    \label{fig:conical-nozzle-loss}

The nozzle is sized with the isentropic choked mass flow equation \cref{eq:choked_massflow}.

\begin{equation}
    \dot{m} = \frac{A_{th}\, p_{01}}{\sqrt{T_{01}}}
    \sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{R}} \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\right)^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}
    \label{eq:choked_massflow}
\end{equation}

% This equation does double duty in this thesis. In design it sizes the nozzle throats for the required mass flow, and in the analysis of \cref{sec:results} it is the only available estimate of the air mass flow, since the rig carries no air flowmeter. Its key property is that it is independent of shaft speed and of everything downstream of the throat, which makes it valid whether or not the rotor is operating as intended.

While the throat sets the mass flow, the ratio of the exit area to the throat area sets the exit Mach number, through the isentropic area relation \cref{eq:area_mach}.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{A_3}{A_{th}} = \frac{1}{M_3}\left[\frac{2}{\gamma+1}\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2}M_3^2\right)\right]^{\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}}
    \label{eq:area_mach}
\end{equation}

% There is no closed form for $M_3$, so \cref{eq:area_mach} is inverted numerically for the supersonic root given the design area ratio. This relation is what links the nozzle geometry to the velocity triangle: the exit Mach number it returns must match the $M_3$ demanded by the inlet swirl of \cref{eq:triangle_inversion,eq:sizing_exit_state}, and the same relation is solved again when the design is re-evaluated for a different working gas, since the area ratio is fixed by the printed hardware but the Mach number and exit velocity are not.



Historically, the optimal nozzle design for a ideal inviscid gas was designed with the Method of Characteristics as well. Rao provides a method to achieve this \cite{raoExhaustNozzleContour1958}. NASA special pubplication 8120 explains that a single canted parabola closely approximates the ideal nozzle contour and should be used for high performance nozzles \cite{LiquidRocketEngine1976}. In this thesis, the nozzle was designed as a simple conical nozzle with a $7^\circ$ degree half angle. It can be seen from \cref{fig:conical-nozzle-loss} that the performance of a conical nozzle for low area ratios lead to a efficiency loss below 2\%.
