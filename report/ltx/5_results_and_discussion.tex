\subsection{Experimental Campaign}

The experimental campaign was conducted in three phases: commissioning, electric motor driven pump testing, then integrated turbine and pump testing. The standalone turbine testing was not conducted due to time constraints. This means the turbine is assessed through its models and through the coupled test, and a standalone brake characterisation is the first item of future work.

\subsection{Electric Motor Driven Pump Testing}

Following objective 1 of the thesis, which is to characterise the head-flow, efficiency and cavitation characteristics, a series of runs were conducted with the pump driven by the electric motor. Five high quality runs were conducted at different motor throttle settings, providing a range of shaft speeds up to 8500 RPM. The head flow curve and cavitation characteristics require different testing profiles.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item To measure the head-flow curve, the outlet valve was swept from fully open to fully closed slowly while the inlet valve was held open to supply the maximum inlet head possibly by the supply pump. This also measures the efficiency curve.
    \item The cavitation characteristics are determined by the minimum inlet pressure before head breakdown or the NPSH required of the pump at different flow rates. This was measured by sweeping the inlet valve from fully open to fully closed at different outlet valve angles.
\end{enumerate}

To speed up data collection, these two tests were combined into a single sequence of valve commands, run at different motor throttle settings. The exact test profile is:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item T+0: Motor throttle is set to a fixed value, outlet valve is fully open $0^\circ$, and the inlet valve is fully open $0^\circ$. The outlet valve then starts closing slowly over 20 seconds.
    \item T+20: At this point, the outlet valve is fully closed. It is then slowly opened to $10^\circ$.
    \item T+25: Once the outlet valve is at $10^\circ$, the inlet valve is swept from fully open to fully closed over 20 seconds.
    \item T+45: The inlet valve is fully closed, and starts opening back up to $0^\circ$ over 5 seconds.
    \item T+50: The outlet valve is stepped to $20^\circ$ and the inlet valve is swept again from fully open to fully closed over 20 seconds.
    \item This repeats until the outlet valve is fully closed at $80^\circ$, where the inlet valve is swept one last time from fully open to fully closed over 20 seconds and the test ends with both valves commanded to fully open again and motor shut down.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{fig:test_overview} shows the raw channels for all five runs laid over each other against time, the outlet pressure, the flow rate, the shaft torque, the speed, and the two valve positions. Each run is a single ESC throttle setting, displaying the whole electric motor driven pump campaign off this one figure. The two valve rows are the test procedure itself. The two valve traces fall on top of each other across all five runs because the sequence was scripted and identical every time, so any difference between runs comes only from the motor throttle setting.

The first thing the data shows is that everything moves together in the direction it should. A higher throttle setting gives a higher steady speed, and the runs separate cleanly from around 3500 to 7500 RPM with no overlap. The outlet pressure, the flow and the torque are all ordered by speed in the same way, the fastest run highest on every channel and the slowest lowest, and none of them cross over. Within the H-Q test (first 20 seconds of each run) the pressure and the flow move in opposite directions, as the outlet valve closes the pressure rises and the flow falls, which is the head flow trade-off that a pump curve summarises shown directly in time before any curve is drawn \cite{dixonFluidMechanicsThermodynamics1998}. Every channel responds to each valve action coherently, pressure rising as flow falls and torque tracking the load, with nothing flat or lagging or fighting the others, which is the first sign that each sensor is behaving and that the runs are clean enough to compare.

The main limitation of the rig is visible straight away, which is that the shaft speed is not held constant through a run. In a standard head flow test the pump is driven by a closed loop speed controler where the speed is held relatively constant while the discharge valve is swept, so the measured curve belongs to one fixed speed \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020, karassikPumpHandbook2008}. Here the pump is driven open loop by a brushless ESC that sets throttle rather than speed, so the pump shaft speed depends on the motor torque speed curve and speed drops whenever torque is increased. The consequence is that a constant speed characteristic cannot be read straight off the traces. %and every point has to be corrected back to the run nominal speed through the affinity laws before the runs mean anything \cite{dixonFluidMechanicsThermodynamics1998, gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. This is why I ran the campaign as five separate throttle settings, each giving a roughly constant speed run, rather than as one controlled sweep.

A second limitation is that all of this happens far below the design speed. The fastest H-Q run reaches about 7700 RPM, roughly a third of the 20,000 RPM the pump was designed for, so every measurement is off design. %Testing this slowly also means a low Reynolds number, and since the loss fraction in a pump falls as Reynolds number rises \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}, the efficiencies measured here sit at the low end of what the machine would reach at speed. The whole campaign is an off design characterisation and that framing carries through the rest of the discussion.

The cavitation part of each run carries its own limitation that the raw data makes obvious. During every inlet sweep the flow drops as the inlet valve closes, which is the flow row dipping in step with the inlet valve, because the single inlet valve lowers the suction pressure and the flow at the same time. A dedicated suction test holds the flow constant while only the suction pressure is reduced \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020, brennenHydrodynamicsPumps2011}, so the coupling here means the pure suction behaviour at a fixed flow cannot be isolated and the breakdown has to be defined against a moving reference rather than a fixed one.

Finally the pressure and flow traces are noticeably noisy. This is not a measurement artifact. Rather, the scatter is consistent with partial emission operation, where the impeller blade gets near the outlet the velocity slightly increases. This is a known source of pressure unsteadiness in this kind of pump \cite{barskeDesignOpenImpeller}. As Barske explains, altering the number of impeller blades can reduce this fluid vibration. It also means a single sample cannot be trusted the data has to be processed to remove the noise. % and the reduction bins the data and takes a median in each bin before anything is read off it. 

Finally, the torque is not zero at standstill. This is caused by the mechanical seal's static friction producing a torque against the motor's rotor and stator trying to reach equilibrium. While spinning, this effect is removed, so the this oddity can be safely ignored.

\subsection{Data Processing and Uncertainty}

\label{sec:reduction}

To use the raw data to fully characterise the pump at the tested operating conditions, the data has to be processed to remove many effects. First of all, only the points above 75\% of the run's median RPM and throwing away the spin up and spin down. Because the open loop drive lets the speed drift by around 8\% over a single ramp, every surviving point is then corrected back to the median speed which becomes the run reference speed through the affinity laws as described in \cref{sec:theory_scaling}. The head scaled as the square of the speed and the flow scaled linearly with it \cite{lobanoffCentrifugalPumpsDesign1992}. This is the step that turns a run with a drifting speed into a clean characteristic at one reference speed, and it is what makes the five runs in \cref{fig:test_overview} comparable to each other, to the design point and to literature.

The pump is partial emission so the corrected traces are still noisy, around 2\% scatter on head, which is close to the 3\% cavitation breakdown the analysis has to resolve, so no single point can be trusted on its own. To handle this the corrected data are binned in flow, 28 bins for the head curves and 20 for the cavitation sweeps, and within each bin I take the median rather than the mean so that an occasional spike or dropout does not drag the point with it. The scatter that survives the binning is carried as the random uncertainty, taken as the standard error of the median, $1.253\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. This binning pulls the 2\% raw scatter down to under 0.1 m on head, well below the 60\% head collapse that marks breakdown, so the breakdown is a clear cliff and not lost in the noise.

The binned head and flow points, together with the reference speed and the impeller geometry, are then formed into the quantities the rest of the chapter plots. The head and flow are made non dimensional as the Gulich head and flow coefficients, $\psi = 2gH/u_2^2$ and $\phi_2 = Q/(\pi d_2 b_2 u_2)$ \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}, which is the collapse in \cref{fig:dim-hq}. The shaft torque and speed give the input power and the overall efficiency $\eta = \rho g Q H / \tau\omega$, binned in flow the same way, which is \cref{fig:eff-churning}. The suction sweeps are reduced against the inlet pressure to give head against available NPSH and the 3\% breakdown point, which is \cref{fig:npshr}. So the single set of raw channels in \cref{fig:test_overview} feeds every derived figure that follows, through one reduction rather than three.

On top of the random scatter each measured quantity also carries a systematic uncertainty from its instrument, the values listed in \cref{tab:instruments}. The error is propagated with the \texttt{uncertainties} python package. The random and systematic parts are then added in quadrature and the error bars are drawn at twice the combined standard uncertainty, so they are roughly 95\% intervals.% One honest point is that the systematic part is common mode, it shifts a whole curve up or down rather than scattering neighbouring points, so the per point bars slightly overstate how much the points can really move relative to each other. The clean collapse of all five runs onto one non dimensional curve in \cref{fig:dim-hq} is the better evidence that the genuine run to run random scatter is small.


% All error bars in this chapter follow the same simple scheme. The systematic uncertainty of each instrument comes from \cref{tab:instruments}, the random uncertainty is the standard error of the median within each bin, and the two are combined in quadrature at roughly 95\% confidence, $U_Y = \sqrt{B_Y^2 + (2P_Y)^2}$. Propagation into derived quantities like efficiency and the non dimensional coefficients is done by linear propagation. One design decision from \cref{sec:experiment} matters here: because the pressure transducers and the ADC share the same reference voltage, excitation and gain drift cancel to first order and do not appear as a systematic term. Where error bars are not visible they are smaller than the markers.

\subsection{Pump: Extrapolation to Design Speed}

As the pump was only tested to a third of its design speed, the predicted efficiency at the design speed has to be extrapolated from the test data. Following a scaling that was done by NASA in a test of a larger pump of a low specific speed rocket pump took the efficiency from 58.7\% to 68.9\%  \cite{macgregorRotatingPositivedisplacementPumps1974}, the five runs can be used for extrapolation. \cref{fig:eta-re} plots the peak efficiency of each run against speed and fits the loss ratio model $(1-\eta) \propto Re^{-a}$, which is the standard form for friction dominated loss scaling. The fitted exponent is $a = 0.098 \pm 0.010$, essentially the textbook Moody value of 0.1, and extrapolating along the fit gives an efficiency of 26.4\% at \valPumpRpmDesign{} with a band of 25.7 to 27.1\%. The exponent is measured from this machine, not assumed, which is the difference between an extrapolation and a guess. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{results_eta_re_fit.pdf}
    \caption{Peak overall efficiency per run against shaft speed, with the fitted loss ratio $(1-\eta) \propto Re^{-a}$ and its uncertainty band extrapolated to the design speed. The fitted exponent $a = 0.098 \pm 0.010$ matches the textbook friction scaling.}
    \label{fig:eta-re}
\end{figure}

Comparing the measured efficiency of 19\% and extrapolated 26\% to literature shows that the pump here performed at a lower efficiency than CFD predicts. Knyazeva \cite{knyazevaDesignFluidDynamics2021} gives a CFD simulated barske pump of specific speed $n_q=4.7$ at 31\% efficiency. Danieli\cite{danieliConceptualDesignElectric2023} reports that his CFD results for specific speed 9 barske pump gets up to 63\%. Kim's closed impeller design at the same $n_q$ reaches 67\% in CFD, but it is unvalidated and requires 0.31 mm closed channels \cite{kimDevelopmentUltraLowSpecific2022}. Historic measured partial emission pumps from SP-8107 \cite{TurbopumpSystemsLiquid1974}, Balje \cite{baljeBaljeTurbomachinesGuide1981} and \cite{jrHandbookTurbomachinery1995} sit at 35 to 60\% at much larger scale. The gap between this machine and those numbers might be accounted for by the measured churning and the lower Reynolds number, or it could also be an unexplained deficiency in the design.

Carrying the churning multiplier of 2.5 unchanged to \valPumpRpmDesign{} would predict around 3 kW of churning alone, more than the entire turbine power budget. At higher reynolds numbers, this multiplier should fall, and the extrapolation of efficiency to design speed is a more realistic estimate of the machine's performance. The measured churning multiplier is a useful data point for future work, as it is a direct measurement of the open impeller architecture's cost in this machine.

\subsection{Pump: Cavitation}

Cavitation behaviour matters a lot for a rocket pump, since the whole point of the feed system is to run the propellant tanks at low pressure, so the pump has to tolerate a low inlet head without losing its rise. This section characterises where this pump breaks down and, more interestingly, what part of the pump actually causes it.

The suction test is run by holding the speed and the flow rate, then closing the inlet throttle to walk the inlet pressure down. \cref{fig:cav-suction} shows the result, one curve per held flow rate, head normalised by the nominal operating head against the NPSH available. Each curve holds its reference head over most of the suction range and then falls off a cliff. The 3\% head drop criterion picks the breakdown point off each curve, and because the cliff is steep the choice of criterion barely changes the result. %The head loses around 60\% over a range comparable to the binning noise, so the knee and the 3\% line land in effectively the same place.

The low flow curves do this cleanly, holding their reference head over most of the suction range and then falling off a cliff. The high flow curves instead climb as the inlet pressure comes down, the highest flow run reaching about 2.4 times its reference head before the same cliff. That climb is an artifact of the test setup. As closing the inlet throttle also lowers the flow rate, there is around a 34\% drop over a sweep in the higher flows. As seen in \cref{dim-eq}, decreasing flow rate near the cutoff point leads to a higher head, so each curve is the head flow characteristic walked backwards as the valve shuts. A standard suction test would hold the flow fixed and RPM fixed and lower only the inlet pressure, which this single inlet valve cannot do. To remove this effect from the data, each each point will have to be normalized against the non cavitating head at its own flow taken from the measured head flow curve. Or, closed loop control to hold a steady flow rate while lowering the inlet pressure would be needed, which is left as future work.

\subsection{Pump: Video Observations of Cavitation}

Video footage of the 45 and 50 percent ESC throttle were the only ones with quality enough to see the cavitation through the transparent front casing due to an encoding issue with the earlier recordings. The good footage however showed very interesting results. The footage was frame perfect and synchronised to the backend clock to within plus or minus 0.03 seconds. I verified the synchronisation independently by matching the RPM start and stop transitions in the video against the logged data. Both the suction bubble formation and the throat onset can be tracked to frame accurate timing on these two runs. The 40 percent run was already degraded and most of its onset times had to be estimated, and everything below 40 percent was too poor in quality to read, so the analysis rests on the 45 and 50 percent runs with the lower throttles treated as supporting.

%Watching the impeller eye, the cavity is not a single boundary but three concentric zones, read from the centre outward. There is a full air core in the middle, then a layer of bubbles, then an outer bubble edge that forms within milliseconds and marks the boundary against fully wetted water. The state at the eye moves through a clear sequence as suction pressure is lowered: nominal, with all water and no bubbles; onset, where a small bubble first appears against otherwise solid water; an intermediate state, where moving outward from the centre you pass from full air, through bubbles, to water; and full cavitation, where the centre is full air and the remaining annulus is only bubbles. Lowering the inlet pressure grows the cavity, so a lower inlet pressure corresponds directly to a larger bubble radius.

There are two distinct cavitation sites, the impeller suction eye as shown in \cref{fig:cav-eye} and the diffuser throat as shown in \cref{fig:cav-throat} and they behave differently. At high flow, meaning the outlet valve more open, the throat is permanently cavitating: across the runs the throat never clears up to an outlet angle of $50^\circ$. Beyond that point, at lower flow, the throat only begins to cavitate some time after the eye cavity has fully grown, so there seems to be a crossover near an outlet setting of $50^\circ$ between a regime where the throat cavitation is always present and a regime where it onsets within the suction sweep.

The most important thing the video shows is that eye cavitation happens earlier than throat cavitation at low flow, but it does not affect the head the pump produces. A large amount of cavitation can develop in the inlet while the developed head stays flat. The head only collapses once the throat goes. This is the visual confirmation of Lock's claim that a Barske pump is diffuser limited rather than eye limited, and it is something an opaque pump with only a pressure curve could never attribute, because the breakdown and the visible eye cavity are decoupled. A further detail from the same runs is that the developed head actually rises slightly as inlet pressure is lowered, right up until cavitation breaks it.

The video and data both show independently show that the breakdown is not caused by eye cavitation, but by throat cavitation, agreeing with Lock's and Barske's claims.

This NPSHR can be converted into suction specific speed with the \cref{eq:sss}:

the suction specific speed at breakdown from \cref{fig:npshr} is found to be between 45 to 109. That is close to the cavitation onset value of around 64 reported for a Barske type impeller in the same regime \cite{knyazevaDesignFluidDynamics2021}, and far below the industrial figure of around 200. %The reason it is so low is the same finding as above: the throat sets breakdown, not the eye, so the usual eye-limited suction specific speed does not apply.

\subsection{Coupled Turbopump}

The integrated test is the proof of concept of the whole project. With the turbine driving the pump on shop air, the shaft settled at around \valCoupledRpm{} RPM, the pump produced a measurable head rise, and the assembly survived repeated runs. However, the operating point is deep off design, with the stator pressure at roughly 38\% of the design supply and a blade to jet speed ratio of around 0.06 against the design 0.2. \cref{fig:coupled-hq} shows the coupled pump data landing on the motor driven curves, which is the cross validation between the two test configurations.

Without a measured mass flow, a single forward efficiency number for the turbine would be under determined: a choked mass flow with a weak jet and a reduced mass flow with a near design jet both fit the same measured torque. However, at the measured stator inlet pressure of 3.1

The torque models compared against the data in \cref{fig:coupled-torque} are built from the Euler equation with the choked mass flow of \cref{eq:choked_massflow}, which remains valid regardless of the rotor state. For a rotor receiving an effective tangential jet velocity $c_{3u}^{\,e}$, the shaft torque is

\begin{equation}
    \tau = \dot{m} \left(1 + k_R\right) r_m \left(c_{3u}^{\,e} - u\right)
    \label{eq:coupled_torque_model}
\end{equation}

where the factor $(1+k_R)$ carries the contribution of the turned relative flow at exit. The started model sets $c_{3u}^{\,e}$ from the full design expansion at the measured supply pressure, while the unstarted model treats the effective jet as a fraction of it, fitted to the data.

What breaks the degeneracy is the starting analysis from \cref{sec:design}. At the test shaft speed the blade speed is so low that the rotor inlet relative Mach number rises to \valTurbMwTest, above the Goldman starting limit of \valTurbStartLimit, so the rotor passages cannot swallow the startup shock system and the rotor runs unstarted. The design stage prediction was that the rotor cannot start below approximately \valTurbStartRpm{} RPM, and the coupled test never exceeded half of that. An unstarted rotor sits behind a detached bow shock, the passage flow is subsonic, and the jet momentum reaching the blades collapses. \cref{fig:coupled-torque} shows the consequence: the measured torque of around \valCoupledTorque{} sits a factor of four below the started flow model at around \valTurbTorqueStartedTest, and an unstarted model with an effective jet of around a third of the design velocity passes through the data. The near design jet branch of the degeneracy is ruled out precisely because it would require a started rotor, which the starting criterion forbids at this speed. The torque deficit is therefore not an anomaly, it is a diagnosed off design state that the design stage analysis predicted before the test was run.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{results_coupled_torque.pdf}
    \caption{Coupled shaft torque against speed: measured data, the started flow model (dashed) and the unstarted model with a reduced effective jet (solid with band). The factor of four deficit against the started model is explained by the rotor running unstarted below the starting threshold.}
    \label{fig:coupled-torque}
\end{figure}

Two rigour checks defend this diagnosis. First, the nozzle can be ruled out as the location of the loss. At the test supply pressure the over expanded conical nozzle sits at a pressure ratio of about 0.61 to ambient, above the separation threshold of roughly 0.4 from the rocket nozzle separation criteria \cite{starkFlowSeparationRocket2005}, so the nozzle remains attached and supersonic and the velocity collapse must happen at the rotor. % [VERIFY V1] exact test p01 before the viva; near 2.0 bar this verdict weakens
Second, the threshold itself should not be over read. With three discrete nozzles the rotor relative Mach number varies around the admitted arc, so the \valTurbStartRpm{} RPM figure is a mean flow estimate and operation near it may start and unstart intermittently \cite{sudhofDevelopmentTechniquesSupersonic2020}.

Because the stage was never measured in its started state, the design point efficiency prediction has to lean on external evidence, and the literature provides a tight bracket. The closest comparison is a machine of the same architecture, a radial inflow supersonic partial admission impulse stage at Mach 1.65, where the measured debit from ideal efficiency was 28 percentage points \cite{donnellyDesignExperimentalVerification}. The Weiss chain here predicts 65\% falling to \valTurbEtaTsDesign, a debit of just under 28 points, so the loss model magnitude is corroborated experimentally to about one percentage point on the same machine class, with the caveat that his rotor ran started. Peng's 1.5 kW partial admission supersonic stage gives a CFD efficiency of around 42.5\% \cite{pengDesignAnalysis152023}, bracketing the prediction from below. NASA tested a Mach 2 stage from full admission down to 12.5\% admission and found the full admission efficiency at 42.5\% with the drop to 12.5\% admission costing only around 5 points \cite{moffittExperimentalInvestigationPartialadmission1959}, which matters here because it acquits partial admission: at $\varepsilon = \valTurbDoa$ the admission penalty is modest, and the measured deficit belongs to the unstart, not the admission. Ohlsson's closed form partial admission theory, inside whose validation range this turbine's arc to chord ratio sits, composes with his low aspect ratio correlations to a literature only stage efficiency estimate of around 0.60 at his design speed ratio \cite{ohlssonPartialAdmissionTurbines1962, ohlssonLowAspectRatio1964}, a second independent bracket. Finally, a precedent exists for exactly this failure mode: the SLA resin turbine Weiss cites also maxed out below its design speed with the cause left unexplained \cite{weissCustomizedORCMicro2020}. This work supplies the diagnosis that precedent lacked, the combination of supply droop and rotor unstart.

\subsection{Extrapolation to the Design Point}

The design point of \valPumpRpmDesign{} was not reached in testing, so every claim about it is an extrapolation, and the value of this section is being precise about which extrapolations are licensed and which are not. On the pump side the licence is the non dimensional collapse of \cref{fig:dim-hq}. Because five speeds collapse onto one head coefficient curve, the affinity laws demonstrably hold for this machine, and \cref{fig:hq-extrap} carries the pooled curve to the design speed, where it passes through the design point. The efficiency extrapolation rides on the fitted Reynolds exponent of \cref{fig:eta-re} and arrives at 26.4\% with a stated band.

On the turbine side the claim has to be narrower. The coupled test ran at around \valCoupledRpm{} RPM with the rotor unstarted, so the stage similitude parameters at design, the rotor relative Mach number and the blade to jet speed ratio, were never matched. What is validated are the speed independent pieces: the choked nozzle, the MoC profile generation against Goldman's published case, and the starting criterion itself, whose off design prediction the coupled test confirmed. Two named gaps remain on the road to 20,000 RPM. First, the started stage efficiency of \valTurbEtaTsDesign{} is a corroborated prediction, not a measurement, and stays that way until a test above the starting threshold. Second, the working fluid: cold air at $\gamma = 1.4$ stands in for hot gas at around 1.2, which shifts the expansion and the starting margin.

On the adequacy of the toolchain, the verdict is positive within stated limits. Lock's model reproduces the head curve including its droop, and places the cavitation limited runout at the right flow once the as-built throat is used, so it is adequate for design stage screening of this machine class. The Barske sizing model delivered a pump that meets its design head coefficient within 3\%. On the turbine, the speed independent codes validated cleanly, and the loss chain prediction is corroborated externally to about a point, but the started stage remains unverified by this rig. The 1D and analytical toolchain was sufficient to design, predict and diagnose everything this project measured, and nothing in the data demanded CFD.

On the Barske architecture itself, the central question of the thesis, the data supports a measured verdict. The architecture does what it promises at this specific speed: it is manufacturable where the conventional impeller is not, it delivers its design head, and its suction behaviour is dominated by a single identifiable mechanism that a transparent casing can watch. The price is efficiency, with churning measured at two and a half times the textbook estimate eating two thirds of the shaft power at test speed. Whether around a quarter efficiency at design speed is acceptable depends entirely on the application, and for expendable small rocket stages where feed pressure mass dominates, it plausibly is.

One mechanical learning point belongs in the record. The first rig iteration used printed plastic load bearing shafts, which crept and failed under high speed loads, and the redesign moved every rotating load path to metal, a stainless pump shaft and an aluminium turbine shaft. Printed structural parts are acceptable for casings and blading at these loads, but not for shafts.
