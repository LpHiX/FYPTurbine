\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

The design point for this thesis was chosen from the capabilities of the test rig. The design parameters for the pump and turbine is shown in \cref{tab:design_point} and this section explains how each number was chosen. The RPM limit was set by the maximum speed of the Magtrol TM306 inline torquemeter and the AHB-3 hysteris brake at 20,000 RPM. The pressure rise and flow rate was chosen to study the low specific regime. The supply pump was decided to be Hozelock's JET 3000 K7 Jet pump as it was available in the department and had a published performance curve. At \valPumpMdotDesign, the supply pump had a head of around \valSupplyPumpHeadDesign. Then, the head rise of the pump was chosen to be \valPumpPressureDesign for a specific speed of \valPumpNqDesign.

 The chain starts from a reasonable inlet velocity, which fixes the eye diameter $d_0$. The eye is then enlarged to the blade leading edge diameter $d_1$ to allow the deliberate prerotation Barske recommends. The inlet and exit blade heights follow from continuity, and the tip diameter $d_2$ comes from the recovery equation \cref{eq:barske_tip_diameter} with a deliberately conservative recovery coefficient of $c_k = 0.2$. A conservative $c_k$ oversizes the impeller slightly, and the plan was to trim $d_2$ later if the discharge pressure came out too high. The sized values are listed in \cref{tab:pump_design}. The geometry was checked against the constraints: the inlet blade speed stays below 45 m/s, $d_2/d_1 > 1.5$, the NPSH margin against the supply system is positive, and the predicted shaft power of \valPumpPowerDesign{} sits under the 1800 W brake limit.

It should be stated clearly that the sizing used the Barske model, not Lock's. Lock's refined model was adopted later as the analysis model because it predicts the drooping head curve and the cavitation cutoff that the Barske model cannot, and its comparison against the measured data is in \cref{sec:results}. Rearranging Lock's model into a direct sizing tool is left as future work.

\subsubsection{Diffuser}

The diffuser is a single conical passage placed tangentially on the annular casing, following Barske's guidance from \cref{sec:theory_barske_sizing}. The throat was sized for a throat velocity of 0.8 to 0.9 of $u_2$, giving $d_3 = \valPumpDThreeSized$, and the exit area is 3 to 4 times the throat area, giving $d_4 = \valPumpDFourSized$. The throat is one of the two performance critical tolerances of the whole pump, because the entire flow passes through this one small hole and, as Lock's model predicts, the maximum flow rate is set by cavitation at exactly this location. The comparison of this throat sizing against the literature is discussed in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Mechanical Design}

The dominant mechanical load is not torque but radial thrust. Because the pump is partial emission, the pressure field around the impeller is asymmetric, and the net radial force this produces governs the shaft and bearing design rather than efficiency considerations. The radial thrust is estimated with the standard projected area form

\begin{equation}
    F_R = K_R\, \rho g H\, d_2 b_2
    \label{eq:radial_thrust}
\end{equation}

where $K_R$ is an empirical thrust coefficient and $d_2 b_2$ is the projected impeller discharge area \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. For a single tangential collector the unbalance is large compared to a volute pump, and at the design head this force, not the 1.2 Nm of torque, is what loads the bearings. The shaft is a 10 mm solid stainless steel stub, chosen over printed or aluminium options to keep the rotor stiff enough to prevent rotor to stator rubs at the small axial clearance.

The choice of seal came before the model. A rotating shaft has to pass through the casing wall into pressurised water while running at up to \valPumpRpmDesign, and most of the simple options do not survive that. A lip seal wears out fast at this surface speed and holds very little pressure, a clearance or labyrinth seal leaks far too much for a closed water loop where the leakage path is also where the flow is measured, and packing has high friction and needs constant tending. A mechanical face seal is the sensible answer, a spring loaded rotating face running against a stationary face with the only leak path a thin liquid film between two lapped surfaces. The seal fitted is a single spring component seal with a face outer diameter of 28.7 mm, an inner diameter of 20.9 mm and a balance diameter of 19.0 mm, held closed by a 50 N spring. % [VERIFY] seal part designation (59U19?); reconcile the 19.0 mm balance diameter with the 10 mm stub shaft (seal sleeve / stepped seat)
These dimensions give a balance ratio of $b = 1.20$, so the seal is unbalanced and the closing pressure grows faster than the sealed pressure. That is harmless at the low test pressures but it is exactly what drives the heat load at the design point, which is what the friction model is for.

The mechanical seal sets its own requirement, and its friction was modelled rather than guessed because it sits between the torquemeter and the impeller, inside every torque measurement of \cref{sec:results}. The net closing pressure on the seal faces is the sealed pressure acting on the hydraulic unbalance of the face, plus the spring:

\begin{equation}
    p_f = \Delta p \left(b - k\right) + p_{sp}, \qquad
    b = \frac{d_o^2 - d_b^2}{d_o^2 - d_i^2}
    \label{eq:seal_balance}
\end{equation}

where $b$ is the seal balance ratio built from the face outer and inner diameters $d_o$, $d_i$ and the balance diameter $d_b$, $k \approx 0.5$ is the pressure profile factor for the fluid film between the faces, and $p_{sp}$ is the spring force over the face area \cite{gulichCentrifugalPumps2020}. % [VERIFY] cite: balance-ratio formulation is standard seal practice (Karassik/Mayer); swap citation if Gulich lacks it
The friction torque and heat follow as

\begin{equation}
    \tau_{seal} = p_f\, A_f\, r_m\, f, \qquad
    P_{seal} = \tau_{seal}\, \omega
    \label{eq:seal_heat}
\end{equation}

with $A_f$ the face contact area, $r_m$ the mean face radius and $f$ the face friction coefficient. The fitted single spring seal used here has a balance ratio of $b = 1.20$, an unbalanced seal, so the closing pressure grows faster than the sealed pressure. With the dry running textbook coefficient $f = 0.07$, the model predicts about 0.4 Nm and 860 W of face heating at the 20 bar, 20,000 RPM design point, and about 0.08 Nm and 60 W at the achieved test conditions. The design point heat flux would flash the water film at the faces to steam without an active flush, so the seal is flushed by the pumped water itself. Two parts of this model are tested against data later: the static starting torque, quoted by Karassik at 3 to 5 times the running torque, is compared against the measured stiction band in \cref{sec:results}, and the friction coefficient itself is fitted from the measured loss budget, which returns $f$ well below the dry value because the faces run on a water film.

Rolling bearing friction was estimated with the SKF four component model, which sums a load dependent rolling term, a sliding term and, for a submerged bearing, a drag term:

\begin{equation}
    M_{brg} = M_{rr} + M_{sl} + M_{drag}
    = \phi_{ish}\,\phi_{rs}\, G_{rr} \left(\nu_l\, n\right)^{0.6} + G_{sl}\, \mu_{sl} + M_{drag}
    \label{eq:bearing_friction}
\end{equation}

where the $G$ factors carry the bearing geometry and load, the $\phi$ factors are shear heating and starvation corrections, and $\nu_l$ is the lubricant kinematic viscosity \cite{skfRollingBearings}. % [VERIFY] add SKF Rolling Bearings catalogue (model source) to the bib
For the 61900 deep groove bearings running submerged in water, the model predicts roughly 0.2 W per bearing at test speed and under 4 W at the full design speed. Bearing friction is therefore negligible against the seal and churning losses, a conclusion the shutoff loss decomposition of \cref{sec:results} supports.

The front casing and the diffuser were printed in clear resin and polished. This was a deliberate design decision, enabled by the low test pressures, so that cavitation could be observed directly through the casing wall. The payoff of this decision is the cavitation visualisation in \cref{sec:results}, which turned out to be one of the most valuable measurements of the project.

The pump shaft critical speed was bounded with the same overhung rotor estimate used for the turbine shaft, \cref{eq:overhung_critical}. Treating the 10 mm stainless shaft ($E = 193$ GPa, $I = \pi d^4/64 = 4.9\times10^{-10}$ m$^4$) as a cantilever from the bearing with the impeller mass at the overhang, the first bending speed stays above approximately 45,000 RPM for any plausible combination of overhang length up to 50 mm and impeller mass up to 100 g, a margin of better than 2.2 over the design speed. % [VERIFY] as-built overhang length and impeller mass; tighten the bound with the real values
The bound is deliberately conservative, since the short stub geometry was chosen exactly to keep this margin large, as discussed in \cref{sec:experiment}.

\subsubsection{Manufacturing and As-Built}

The impeller, casing and diffuser were printed by SLA. Additive manufacturing is acceptable for this pump precisely because of the forced vortex operation described in \cref{sec:theory}. With the fluid rotating as a solid body, the relative velocity over the blade surfaces is low, so blade surface finish has little effect on performance and the print finish is sufficient. Two tolerances dominate performance and were flagged for inspection: the axial clearance $s_{ax}$ between the blades and the casing, and the diffuser throat diameter. The as-built throat turned out to matter a great deal, as the true minimum area was found to be the \valPumpThroat{} semicircular passage into the annular casing rather than the \valPumpThroatCutwater{} cone cutwater downstream, and this distinction becomes central to the cavitation analysis in \cref{sec:results}. Full as-built deviation tables are in the appendix.

\subsubsection{Comparison to Conventional Design}

Before committing to the Barske architecture, a conventional shrouded impeller was carried through a full Kaplan method blade construction at this duty point. The attempt is documented in \cref{sec:appendix_conventional} and its conclusion is what justifies the architecture choice. The design correlations repeatedly broke down outside their validity ranges, and the geometry that emerged had twisted multi streamline blades with sub millimetre closed channel widths, which is not manufacturable at this scale with any process available to this project. This is consistent with the published conventional design at the same specific speed, which required 0.31 mm closed channels \cite{kimDevelopmentUltraLowSpecific2022}. The quantitative comparison of the two architectures against literature is made with the measured data in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Turbine Design}

\subsubsection{Design Point and Regime Shift}

Turbine sizing has to satisfy many requirements simultaneously, many design variables. After the pump was sized for \valPumpRpmDesign at a power of \valTurbPowerDesign, there are many remaining free variables.

The main parameters being turbine diameter $D_mean$, mass flow through the turbine $\dot{m}$, blade inlet angle $\beta_3$.

One thing has to be kept honest throughout this section and the results. The turbine has two operating regimes. The design point is \valTurbPowerDesign{} at \valPumpRpmDesign{} on \valTurbPZeroOneDesign{} supply. The test point is what the laboratory shop air could actually deliver, around 2.9 bar absolute at the stator with the pressure drooping under flow, which settled the coupled shaft at around \valCoupledRpm{} RPM. \cref{tab:turbine_design_vs_test} compares the two. The choked supersonic nozzle behaves the same in both regimes because choked flow is speed independent, but the rotor stage similitude parameters were not matched at test, and this difference drives most of the discussion in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Architecture Selection}

The stage is an impulse stage, for the reasons established in \cref{sec:theory}: at low blade to jet speed ratio the impulse stage is more efficient than a reaction stage, and it is far more tolerant of tip clearance, which matters when the rotor is printed \cite{LiquidRocketEngine1974}. The central constraint is diameter. Running the stage at the optimum blade to jet speed ratio on a 20,000 RPM shaft would need a mean diameter in the hundreds of millimetres, which does not fit the rig or the shaft dynamics, so the turbine accepts a small diameter and a heavily off optimum speed ratio instead. The small mass flow then cannot fill the annulus at any sensible blade height, so partial admission is used. The degree of admission $\varepsilon = \valTurbDoa$ was chosen by working \cref{eq:turbine_blade_height} backwards, picking the admission fraction that raises the blade height to \valTurbBladeHeight, the smallest height that prints reliably and keeps the aspect ratio sensible.

\subsubsection{Sizing}

The mean line sizing is the inverse design chain of the theory chapter, executed in order. The inputs are the shaft power $P$, speed $n$, mean diameter $d_m$, mass flow $\dot{m}$, nozzle angle $\beta_3$, degree of admission $\varepsilon$, the gas properties and the exit pressure $p_e$. The blade speed follows from the speed and diameter, $u = \omega d_m/2$, and the required specific work from the power, $\Delta h_{\textrm{use}} = P/\dot{m}$. The velocity triangle inversion \cref{eq:triangle_inversion} then fixes the jet the nozzle must deliver, $c_{3u}$, $c_3$ and $c_{3m}$. The nozzle exit static state follows from conservation of energy in the adiabatic nozzle and the isentropic relations:

\begin{equation}
    T_3 = T_{01} - \frac{c_3^2}{2 c_p}, \qquad
    a_3 = \sqrt{\gamma R T_3}, \qquad
    M_3 = \frac{c_3}{a_3}
    \label{eq:sizing_exit_state}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    p_3 = \frac{p_{01}}{\left(1 + \frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_3^2\right)^{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}}}, \qquad
    \rho_3 = \frac{p_3}{R T_3}
    \label{eq:sizing_exit_pressure}
\end{equation}

Because the stage is impulse, the rotor takes no static pressure drop, so the nozzle exit static pressure must equal the exit pressure $p_e$. The supply pressure $p_{01}$ is therefore not free: it is iterated until $p_3 = p_e$, which is how the design tool arrives at the required stator supply pressure of \valTurbPZeroOneDesign{} rather than assuming it. The blade height then follows from continuity through the admitted arc, \cref{eq:turbine_blade_height}, and the total nozzle throat area from the choked flow relation \cref{eq:choked_massflow}. Finally the Weiss loss chain of \cref{subsec:weissloss} is wrapped around this ideal chain in an outer loop: the internal power is raised until the shaft power net of the rotor loss and ventilation meets the \valTurbPowerDesign{} target, so the quoted supply pressure already carries the losses.

With the chain in place, the remaining free choices are the mean diameter and mass flow. These were chosen by sweeping the $(d_m, \dot{m})$ plane with the full model and overlaying the manufacturing and rig constraints: a minimum blade height of 4 mm for reliable printing, a minimum nozzle throat width of 0.5 mm, and a nozzle exit Mach ceiling of 1.8 to keep the rotor inside the validated range of the loss correlations. \cref{fig:turbine-design-space} shows the swept design space, the feasible region, and the selected design point. The chosen $d_m = \valTurbDmean$ and $\dot{m} = \valTurbMdotDesign{} $ sit inside the feasible region with the blade height constraint binding, which is the quantitative version of the statement that the blade height, not the aerodynamics, sizes this machine.

\subsubsection{Nozzle}

The ideal nozzle contour for this application would be a Rao or canted parabola design as described in \cref{sec:theory}. Due to time constraints and the negligible performance difference at this low area ratio, a simple conical nozzle with a 7 degree half angle and rounded corners was used instead. NASA SP-8120 puts the efficiency penalty of a conical contour below 2\% at low area ratios, which is small against the other losses in this stage, so this is an honest engineering trade rather than a compromise of the results. There are \valTurbNozzles{} nozzles with a total throat area of \valTurbAthroat.

\subsubsection{Blade Passage Design}

The blade passage was generated with the MoC procedure of \cref{subsec:method_of_characteristics} for an inlet relative Mach number of 1.3 at a blade angle of 75 degrees from axial, with surface Mach numbers $M_l = 1.05$ and $M_u = 1.5$. The choice of this surface Mach pair is the central design decision of the blade. The pair is not free: their mean is fixed near the inlet Mach number and their spread sets the turning the stage needs. The lower bound is separation on the pressure surface, checked with the Sasman and Cresci method of \cref{sec:theory}, and the upper bound is starting, checked with the Goldman criterion derated by the Colclough fit. Following Goldman's observation that efficiency rises with Mach spread, the spread was opened until separation became imminent. \cref{fig:moc-design} shows the result: the as-designed blade with the $H_i$ check touching 2.1 at around 77\% chord on the upper surface, deliberately separation imminent at the design point. The boundary layer displacement correction applied to the printed contour is shown in \cref{fig:moc-displaced}.

The starting margin was left deliberately thin to maximise loading. The Goldman limit for this passage is $M_{w3} = \valTurbStartLimit$ against a design value of \valTurbMwDesign. The same calculation run off design predicted, before any coupled test took place, that the rotor cannot start below approximately \valTurbStartRpm{} RPM, because at low blade speed the relative inlet Mach number rises above the limit. This prediction is a design stage statement. What happened when the coupled test ran far below this threshold is the subject of \cref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Losses and Predicted Efficiency}

Applying the Weiss loss chain of \cref{subsec:weissloss} at the design point gives a nozzle velocity coefficient of $k_N = \valTurbKnDesign$, a rotor velocity coefficient of $k_R = \valTurbKrDesign$, and a ventilation power of \valTurbPwDesign. Together these take the ideal stage efficiency of around 65\% down to a predicted total to static efficiency of \valTurbEtaTsDesign. As one external sanity check, the roughly 28 percentage point debit from ideal that this chain predicts matches the debit measured experimentally on a machine of the same architecture \cite{donnellyDesignExperimentalVerification}, which is examined properly in \cref{sec:results}.

\subsubsection{Blade Geometry Details}

Three geometry details depart from the ideal MoC contour. First, the leading edge. The MoC construction produces a zero thickness leading edge, which cannot be printed. A finite wedge of about 5 degrees was added on the pressure surface following the approach in \cref{sec:theory}. The shock detachment angle at the design relative Mach number of about 1.3 is around 6.5 degrees, so a 5 degree wedge keeps the oblique shock attached. Second, the trailing edge is truncated to a flat of about 0.8 mm so it survives both the shock loading and embrittlement, since the expansion cools the air to around $-88\,^\circ$C at the exit. Third, balance holes are drilled through the disc web to equalise the pressure across the rotor and remove around 400 N of parasitic axial thrust. Balance holes and pistons are standard rocket pump practice \cite{kawasakiUnsteadyResponseFlow2013}, and a CFD study of a Barske type pump in the same regime showed balance holes reducing the axial force from 17 kN to 350 N, the same order of effect as needed here \cite{knyazevaDesignFluidDynamics2021}. Finally, the blades are shrouded, which reduces the blade tip deflection by a factor of around 48 and removes tip leakage as a loss mechanism.

\subsubsection{Cold Flow Thermal Environment}

Expanding room temperature air to Mach \valTurbMThreeDesign{} cools it to a static temperature of around 185 K, and the printed resin blades have to survive this. The static temperature is however not what the blade surface sees. A surface in high speed flow recovers part of the kinetic energy as frictional heating in the boundary layer, and equilibrates at the adiabatic wall temperature

\begin{equation}
    T_{aw} = T_3 + r\, \frac{w^2}{2 c_p}, \qquad r = \mathrm{Pr}^{1/3}
    \label{eq:recovery_temperature}
\end{equation}

where $r$ is the recovery factor for a turbulent boundary layer and $w$ is the local relative velocity \cite{andersonCompressible}. % [VERIFY] cite: any compressible-flow text carries the turbulent recovery factor; match to the bib
With $\mathrm{Pr} \approx 0.70$ for cold air, $r \approx 0.89$, and at the design rotor inlet relative velocity the recovery term adds back around 58 K, so the blade surfaces equilibrate near $-30\,^\circ$C rather than the static $-88\,^\circ$C.

Two follow up checks close the question. First, the blade thermal time constant. Treating the thin blade as a lumped capacitance with the film coefficient from a turbulent flat plate correlation evaluated at the blade chord, the time constant $\tau_{th} = \rho_b\, (t/2)\, c_{p,b} / h$ comes out below one second, so the blades reach their steady temperature within seconds of the start of a run and there is no transient grace period to exploit. Second, dimensional stability: the thermal strain at 50 K below ambient with the resin's expansion coefficient of order $10^{-4}$ per K is about 0.6\%, which over the 4 mm blade height is some 25 \textmu m, small against the running clearances. The thermal design conclusion is therefore that the geometry is unaffected but the material toughness at $-30\,^\circ$C is the binding concern, which is what motivated the truncated trailing edge flat of the previous section.

\subsubsection{Structural and Rotordynamic Analysis}

The rotor was checked with static FEA at the design speed: blade root bending under the combined centrifugal and gas loads, blade deflection, shroud hoop stress and disc bore stress. The blade bending safety factor is \valTurbBladeSf{} on the Tough 2000 material. All FEA was run in Fusion 360 static studies. For linear elastic statics the solver brand is irrelevant, and each result was cross checked against a hand calculation of the matching idealised case, which is the verification that actually matters. % mesh + convergence statement to add with fig:turbine-fea

The hand calculations are the classical rotating component results. The peak hoop stress of a solid rotating disc bounds the disc and bore stress,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\theta,max} = \frac{3+\nu}{8}\, \rho_b\, \omega^2 r^2
    \label{eq:disc_hoop}
\end{equation}

the centrifugal pull at the blade root is the integrated body load of the blade above it,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{root} = \frac{\rho_b\, \omega^2}{2} \left(r_{tip}^2 - r_{root}^2\right)
    \label{eq:blade_pull}
\end{equation}

and the shroud ring carries its own centrifugal load as a free hoop,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{hoop} = \rho_b\, u_{shroud}^2
    \label{eq:shroud_hoop}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_b$ is the blade material density and $u_{shroud}$ the shroud rim speed. The gas bending load on each blade adds a cantilever bending stress $\sigma_b = M c / I$ at the root, with the moment from the blade force of the velocity triangles. Each FEA peak stress was required to agree with its idealised counterpart to the level the idealisation allows, which catches unit, constraint and load errors that mesh refinement cannot.

For the shaft, the finite element rotordynamics of \cref{fig:turbine-campbell} is cross checked by the overhung rotor estimate. Treating the shaft as a cantilever of bending stiffness $EI$ with the disk mass $m$ at radius $L$ from the bearing,

\begin{equation}
    \omega_1 \approx \sqrt{\frac{3EI}{m L^3}}
    \label{eq:overhung_critical}
\end{equation}

which lands in the same regime as the finite element first bending pair and confirms the order of the margin.

For rotordynamics, the shaft is a 10 mm solid aluminium stub. A finite element rotor model with the turbine disk overhung and the measured disk mass and inertias places the first bending natural frequency at standstill around 99,000 RPM, and the Campbell diagram in \cref{fig:turbine-campbell} shows the margin over the full operating range. The first synchronous crossing sits far above the 20,000 RPM design speed, with a margin of better than 3.5. The shaft connects to the pump through a jaw coupler with Double-D 7 mm flats, sized with a compressive safety factor of around 10 on the printed hub.

\subsubsection{Manufacturing}

The rotor and the stator nozzle block were printed by SLA in Tough 2000 resin, washed in IPA, UV cured, and the mating faces sanded flat. Printed resin turbomachinery at this power level is published practice rather than an improvisation, as Weiss cites both an experimental kW scale SLA resin air turbine and SLA diffuser vanes in operation \cite{weissCustomizedORCMicro2020}.
